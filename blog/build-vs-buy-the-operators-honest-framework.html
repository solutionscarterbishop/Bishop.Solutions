Build vs. buy: the operator's honest framework

Most build-vs-buy conversations get stuck comparing licensing costs against engineering costs. That's the wrong axis. The real axis is fit — how deeply this system needs to reflect the way this specific business actually runs.

The bad framework: cost vs. cost

The default framework is dollars against dollars. Vendor license and setup vs. developer time and maintenance. It looks clean on a slide and it hides the actual question.

Cost math is easy and wrong because it treats the two paths as substitutes. They're not. They produce different systems.

The better framework: fit, leverage, longevity

Three questions do most of the work:

  1. Fit: Does the way we do this differ meaningfully from how the market does it? If yes, forcing the business into someone else's model is expensive in ways that never show up on the invoice.
  2. Leverage: Would owning this system compound — because it feeds other systems, or because it's central to how customers experience us? If yes, building is a strategic asset. If not, buying is a shortcut worth taking.
  3. Longevity: Will this workflow still exist in three years? If it's transient, don't build it. If it's core, don't rent it.
Buy commodity, build advantage.

The rules of thumb I actually use

  • Buy anything that looks like everyone else's version. Email, calendars, video calls, generic project boards, most accounting. Don't be interesting here.
  • Build anything downstream of your operating advantage. The pipeline, the CRM, the workflow that makes your business feel different — those deserve to be shaped by you.
  • Own the data model even if you don't own the app. If you buy, make sure the data comes out clean. Vendors change; your data model shouldn't.
  • "Build" doesn't mean "from scratch." A thin custom layer on top of the right primitives is often the right move.

Real examples from my desk

Bought: email, calendars, video, most infrastructure primitives. Built: Bishop CRM, Review Intelligence, Marketing Command Center, internal SOP and reporting tooling. The pattern is consistent: rent commodity, own advantage.

What people get wrong

  • Underestimating the drag of a bad-fit vendor. Every hour spent working around the tool is a hidden cost.
  • Overestimating build cost. Modern tooling makes small, purpose-built apps far cheaper than they used to be.
  • Confusing complexity with sophistication. The best internal tool is usually the simplest one that fits.
